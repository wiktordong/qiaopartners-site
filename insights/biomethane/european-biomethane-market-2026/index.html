Biomethane at 8 bcm: how far Europe has come and how far it has to go

Installed capacity grew 17% in a year and investment commitments reached €36 billion. Even if every announced project is built, Europe reaches about half of its 2030 target.

Wiktor Dong, Qiao Partners8 November 20264 min read

Key points

  • Europe had 1,975 biomethane plants and 8.2 bcm/year of capacity at the end of Q2 2026, after 327 plants were commissioned in twelve months.
  • Announced investment of €36 billion would add about 9 bcm by 2030, leaving roughly 18 bcm of the 35 bcm target without an identified project.
  • Five countries produce 95% of output. The next phase of growth will come from new markets and from multi-plant platforms.

The European Biogas Association and Gas Infrastructure Europe published the 2026 European Biomethane Map on 1 July. It shows 8.2 bcm a year of installed capacity at the end of the second quarter, an increase of more than 1 bcm, or 17%, on the previous year. The number of operating plants rose from 1,678 to 1,975, with 327 new plants entering operation. The plant count has roughly quadrupled since 2018.

A larger and more industrial fleet

The average European biomethane plant now produces about 472 Nm³/h. That is several times the size of the farm biogas engines that dominated the previous decade, and it reflects a shift from electricity generation on site to upgrading and injection into the gas grid.

The wider biogas sector is much bigger than the biomethane fleet. Combined biogas and biomethane output in Europe was 22 bcm in 2024, 19 bcm of it in the EU, according to the EBA's Statistical Report. Most of that biogas is still burned in engines to make power and heat. Each of those sites is a candidate for conversion to grid injection, which is often cheaper and faster than a greenfield project.

Leadership has also changed. France overtook Germany as Europe's largest biomethane producer, on the back of a feed-in tariff that made small and mid-sized injection plants bankable.

The arithmetic of 2030

The EBA's 2026 Investment Outlook counts €36 billion of committed investment, up from €28 billion a year earlier. If all of it is spent, it adds about 9 bcm of annual capacity by 2030. Added to the installed base, that takes Europe to roughly 17 bcm, about half of the 35 bcm REPowerEU target.

Feedstock does not appear to be the limit. A 2026 Guidehouse study for the EBA puts the EU's sustainable potential at 31–32 bcm by 2030 and up to 184 bcm by 2050. The binding constraints are permitting time, grid connection, revenue certainty and the capacity of developers and contractors to build. The EBA also notes that five countries account for 95% of European production. Most member states have barely started.

Where the growth will come from

The first wave of European biomethane was led by farmers and driven by national subsidy. We expect the next wave to look different: portfolios of 10 to 50 plants owned by infrastructure funds and utilities, built to standard designs, run from central control rooms, and earning revenue from several sources at once. Spain's Asterion Industrial Partners, which plans €1.5 billion for a pan-European platform targeting 20 operating plants by the end of 2026, is an example of the model.

Outlook

At the current build rate Europe will add perhaps 1–1.5 bcm a year. Reaching 35 bcm by 2030 would require something closer to 6 bcm a year. The target will probably be missed, but the size of the shortfall still matters to investors: it means demand for new capacity, and for the certificates it generates, will exceed supply through the end of the decade. We see the most attractive entry points in the early markets, where policy has recently been put in place and competition for sites and feedstock is still limited.
